P3B	A forest does not have one face. There is the green at your feet that you have to bend your knees to see; there is the white wood of winter, which only brightens once it has put every leaf down; and there is the jungle that climbs in layers all the way to a glass sky. The three cafés in this part bring you one of those faces each. Trees live far longer and far more slowly than we do, and sitting beside them tends to slow a person down without their consent. By the time you have finished a coffee and got up to leave, your breathing will be gentler than it was on the way in.
P6H	One Color, Another Season
P6B	We live inside the settings the world has chosen for us. Streets are grey, summer is hot, winter is cold. And here are two doors that turn the whole grammar over. Open the first and the light of noon comes down on you even on an overcast day; open the second and a snowfield opens out in the middle of August. One color and one temperature. Only those two were reversed, and behind each door an entirely different kind of day begins.
P7H	Trying On Another Life
P7B	A person gets one life and lives it. But certain rooms let you borrow somebody else's for an afternoon. These two doors do exactly that. Open the first and you become a bee walking into a golden six-sided room; open the second and you are a sailor waiting by a fire for the tide. A borrowed life comes off again in half a day, and yet the warmth of having worn it stays strangely long.
P8H	On the Things We Store
P8B	People have been putting things aside for a very long time. To get through a winter, to survive a bad harvest, to be ready for a day that has not come yet. The impulse that filled the granary and buried the jar and raised the warehouse was fear, and it was also hope, because only a creature that believes in tomorrow declines to eat today. This door opens onto a place like that. Into the enormous vessel where a city kept its future.
P9H	On the Things We Built to Outlast Us
P9B	We are the only animal that knows it has an end, and therefore the only animal that has tried to build a forever. This last door borrows the shape of the largest thing our species ever stacked toward eternity, and tells the opposite story with it: that eternity was nowhere to be found, and that the afternoon is always right here. It is the twenty-first door, and the last. Once it closes behind you, the street you walk every day will look a little different.
SILO	Duck back through the small steel door and the harbor wind is on your face. Behind you, eight great cylinders stand in a row, and out of one of them the sound of people laughing is still coming. On a day when your life is full and you are strangely hungry anyway, remember this: there is a vessel on this harbor that is largest because it is empty. And sitting inside it, quietly taking stock of your own granary, costs a cup of coffee.
