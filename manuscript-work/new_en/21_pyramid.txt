H2	The Café of Forever: Pyramid
B	The passage narrows a little, and darkens a little. You walk perhaps twenty paces along it, past hieroglyphs cut into the wall and moving in the low lamplight, and then the walls fall away and an enormous emptiness opens up. Four stone faces rise inward at a slant and meet at a single point far overhead: you are inside a square pyramid. Slits of light are set into the sloping walls like stars, and the cool silence of stone settles on your shoulders. A minute ago you were standing on a street with buses on it. This is Pyramid, a café where a cup of coffee buys you a pharaoh's afternoon.
B	Pyramid stands on the western edge of the city, out where the low hills run. Get off at the last bus stop, walk up a gentle road, and there is a sandstone pyramid sitting on the hilltop. It is about as tall as a three-story building, but with nothing near it to measure against, it stands a great deal larger than that. Sunset is this building's hour. When the western sky reddens, the silhouette of the pyramid rises black against it, and that view has made this hill the best evening walk in the city. It is also why the window seats on the homeward bus fill up on the hill side first.
B	Up close the wall has the grain of large sandstone blocks laid in courses. Put a hand on it and you get the rough, solid feel of the real thing. There is no sign of any size. On the wall beside the door there is an oval cut into the stone with the name of the house inside it, in a typeface that leans toward hieroglyphic. Anyone who knows that the old kings protected their names by carving them inside an oval will smile once in front of that nameplate. This house enshrined its own name like a king before it did anything else.
H3	The way in to the king's chamber
B	The passage inside the door is the threshold rite of the house. The floor is level, but the walls and ceiling narrow steadily and the light drops as you go, so the body quiets down of its own accord while you walk. The reliefs along the wall are a procession of birds and eyes and boats and suns. You do not need to know what they say. The sense of passing between old things is the entire point. At the end of the passage a member of staff nods and extends a hand, and the world that has been closing in opens all at once into a great volume of air. Compression, then release. This house took the oldest piece of staging the tombs of kings ever knew and used it as the threshold of a café.
H3	A sky of stone with stars set into it
B	The sloping walls own the room. Four faces of stone rise inward toward a single point, and everything in this space comes from that angle. The eye runs up the wall without being asked, and the mind gathers somewhere under the apex. Thin slits are cut through the stone here and there and glitter through the day like constellations — a version of the shafts said to have been bored northward toward a star from the king's chamber of the real thing. And around sunset, light comes through a slit in the western wall as a long gold column, crossing the hall at an angle and moving slowly across the floor. The evening customers of this house wait quietly for the few minutes when that column passes their own table.
B	The light overall is low and golden. Lamps set along the walls wash up the grain of the stone from below so that the whole hall glows faintly, like the inside of old metal. It is lavish without being bright, grand without being cold. Thick stone walls leave the noise and the heat and the cold of the outside standing at the door, so the room keeps the temperature of stone in every season, neither warm nor cool. In that quiet, only the sound of a cup being set down and the low run of conversation come through clearly.
B	The seating runs low around the walls. Under the slant the ceiling comes down into snug alcoves, with deep-backed chairs and low stone tables. In the middle of the hall, directly beneath the apex, there is one large round table. It is the most open seat in the house and the most sought after, because sitting at the center of a room whose four walls are all leaning in toward you is not an experience on sale anywhere else.
H3	This treasure cannot be robbed
B	Pyramid's design principle lies in changing the material of magnificence. The chambers of kings were magnificent in gold; this room is magnificent in light. Gilt has been replaced by golden lamps, jewels by slits of star, wall paintings by a column of sunset. Which means the treasure of this pyramid cannot be taken by robbers, because the treasure is light. Building the grandeur of a king's chamber out of two things, the grain of stone and the angle of light, is this house's real craft — and it has the side effect that all this splendor costs almost nothing to maintain. The sun delivers a new treasure every day and never sends an invoice.
H3	A light back on at the jeweler's bench
B	In the parade of shops at the bottom of the hill there is a jeweler, forty years old, who used to engrave first-birthday rings and wedding bands. As the age of formal gifts faded, the light over his bench was off more days than it was on. What Pyramid asked him for, when it opened, was cartouches: small plaques carrying a customer's name transliterated into hieroglyphs inside an oval, like the nameplate on the wall outside. The old engraver spent a night going through the reference material and came back the next day with three samples instead of an answer.
B	The light over his bench is on again now. For a baby's hundredth day, for the day an exam result comes through, for a colleague's last morning before retirement, people come down the hill to have a name cut. He adjusts his eyeglass and laughs. "Forty years of engraving rings, and now I engrave the names of kings. Turns out engraving names is the job I'd been doing all along." A leather workshop and a stamp-carver's have taken two of the empty units in the parade, and people have started calling it the engraving lane. A light back on over a bench, new workshops, a new name for the street. As evidence that a fading parade woke up as a lane of handwork, that's enough.
H3	How to drink like a pharaoh
B	The thing Pyramid is known for is the gold latte: honey and spice let down in milk to a golden color, served with a single very thin leaf of edible gold laid across it like a sunset. People climb the hill for the extravagance of that gold touching the lip on the first mouthful. Beside it is the Nile cake, dates and nuts chopped and layered, and cut open it shows the strata of a river's deposits. The story of how the silt a river carried down each year fed a civilization is a thing this cross-section tells you with one fork.
B	And every order comes with a cartouche card, a small card that renders the sound of your name in hieroglyphs. There are stamps beside the till, and the customer prints their own name inside the oval. The expression on a person's face as they first look down at their name assembled out of birds and water and eyes is this café's favorite thing in the world. Those ovals travel around the city in wallets and between the pages of books. What the kings used to do, everybody does now — which is to leave a name behind.
H3	"Sitting here makes my problems look like artifacts"
B	A startup developer in her twenties who books the round table once a month explains what she comes for. "Sitting here makes my problems look like artifacts. When I think about people thousands of years ago building this because they wanted to live forever, this quarter's targets suddenly look adorable. I come whenever I start to feel rushed. I'm here to borrow the unit of time these walls use."
B	An evening regular in his thirties comes for a few minutes of sunset. "The light comes through the slit in the west wall and a gold column crosses the hall. Takes about ten minutes. While it's passing my table I don't do anything. Phone face down. I learned here that ten minutes a day, if you actually keep them, changes the week."
H3	I Was Given a Pharaoh's Afternoon
B	A travel writer who has spent a career filing from ancient sites opened his piece on Pyramid like this. "I have been to a real pyramid. I queued in desert heat, crawled bent double up a robber's shaft, looked at an empty king's chamber for three minutes, and came out. It was magnificent and it was punishing. And then on a hill in this city I sat in a king's chamber for two hours, drinking a gold latte in the cool silence of stone, looking up at a sky of rock with stars cut into it. A king four and a half thousand years ago acquired this room by dying. I acquired it for the price of a coffee, alive. I laughed at that piece of arithmetic for a long time."
B	His entry closes: "Go to this café an hour before sunset. Watch the column of light cross the hall, and print the cartouche of your own name on the way out. For the price of a coffee you will be given an afternoon that has no reason to envy eternity."
H3	The unopened things in the boxes
B	The woman who opened Pyramid is someone who has cleared out her parents' belongings. After the funeral, when she finally opened the wardrobe in the long-empty house, it was not the worn things that undid her. It was the new ones. Bedding still in its wrapping, a scarf never once worn, good dishes saved for later, stacked up in their boxes. They had been people who kept the good things back. People who assumed the good day was always somewhere further on. She sat down on the floor in front of those boxes and learned that whatever you save gets left to somebody else.
B	After that winter her arithmetic changed. Use the good cup today, see the good people today, appoint today as the good day. When she decided to open a café, she chose to borrow the shape of the largest building humanity ever put up for the sake of later — in order to sell people the present inside it. "The kings built this shape for eternity," she says. "I built it for an afternoon. Nobody in here is waiting for anything. That is what I am proud of."
H3	What Pyramid actually sells
B	Look at Pyramid's books and it becomes clear that this café is not selling coffee. What it sells is a change of scale. The moment you sit inside a form that has held for thousands of years, your day and your worries get measured with a different ruler. This week's deadline and this month's numbers shrink to their real size in front of a four-thousand-year wall. That shift of scale is not a product any consulting room sells, which is why the round table on this hill does not go in and out of fashion.
B	It resists copying because of the stubbornness of the form. The inside of a square pyramid is about half waste. A great deal of floor area is lost as the walls lean in, and the converging grandeur exists only as the payment for accepting that loss. A business planned by the square meter cannot imitate this room. What brings people back is collecting, and light. Families cut one cartouche after another, name by name; the sunset column arrives at a different angle every season. Because the summer column and the winter column cross different parts of the floor, the regulars move seats as the year goes round.
H3	Buying a civilization whole
B	The queue at Pyramid sits on a shift you could call buying a civilization whole. People have spent a long time looking at artifacts through glass. Three hours in a museum is a reverent business, but your legs hurt and the coffee is only sold in the lobby. Now people want to stay inside a civilization rather than view it: half a day sitting in that period's light, drinking that period's flavors, printing their own name in that period's writing. Meeting a civilization through the body rather than through information. Pyramid is this city's answer to that appetite, and rather than competing with the museum it has become a door into it, since the people who spend half a day here go straight out and look up ticket prices.
H3	Build the last room first
B	The first thing this pyramid has to say to anyone dreaming of a room of their own concerns the order of design. A pyramid is architecture designed from the king's chamber outward. The place where the heart would lie was fixed first, and several million blocks were then stacked in the direction of that room. A shop is the same. Decide first what emotional room the customer is going to arrive at last, and then make the door and the passage and the seating all lead there. That every one of the twenty-one houses in this book was built in that order is something the last of them summarizes with its own body.
B	The nerve to accept waste is worth chewing over as well. The floor area lost to a leaning wall is written into the accounts as a loss, and into the customer's memory as the whole of the place. There was no clean opening for this pyramid either. For the first few months people called it magnificent, took photographs and got up after half an hour. It was only once the low seats under the walls and the round table went in that people began to stay. Grandeur brings people in; what keeps them is somewhere to sit. That tuition fee became this house's second foundation stone.
H3	Why a pyramid makes us better behaved
B	Go further in and you reach the long spell this shape casts. A pyramid is a mountain made by people. As a person is made humble in front of a mountain, in front of a mountain that people piled up we are made humble twice: once by the size of it, and once by the years in the hands that stacked it. And that humility is strangely comfortable. Being told you do not have to be the center of the universe is an unexpected holiday for anyone worn out from playing the lead in their own life every day. That is why the customers under these leaning walls all go gentle. The size of the stone takes over carrying the size of the heart for a while.
B	And this is the least collapsible shape humanity knows. Wide at the bottom and narrow at the top is the honest conclusion of stacking anything, which is why a child's blocks and a sandcastle both end up here. The reassurance inside a pyramid is the reassurance of that honesty. Nothing in here falls down. Nothing has, for several thousand years.
H3	A name laid from the bottom course up
B	Pyramid's history has been written in the evenings of a hill. People climb it every evening for the sunset walk that made it famous, and some of them push open the door of the pyramid for the first time. Couples who have cut cartouches have started printing two ovals side by side on their wedding invitations, and cutting a first cartouche for a baby's hundredth day has become a new custom in this city. On the day before a large decision, people now tell each other they are going up the hill. Everyone here knows which room of stone that means.
B	A great brand is like a pyramid. It is built by the width of the bottom course rather than the sharpness of the top, and what you see is the summit, while what holds the summit up is several million things underneath that nobody sees. And what has been stacked properly is still standing when the age has changed.
H3	What are we deferring today for?
B	Here is a last question worth asking under a sky of stone. What are we putting today off in order to leave behind? The kings spent the working years of whole nations trying to leave eternity. And look at what was left. The mummies decayed, the treasure was scattered, and what actually crossed the thousands of years turns out to be something else entirely: the graffiti the workmen left behind the walls, the ledgers recording their rations of bread and beer, the ordinary days of the nameless people buried beside the monument. What survived from architecture built for eternity was, in the end, the traces of a life being lived. Eternity was never the goal. It was the by-product.
B	A full life might be one that buys an afternoon rather than a forever. Take the good cup out today, call the face you want to see today, write today the line you keep deferring. Days spent carefully like that stack up among themselves, unasked, into a pyramid belonging to one person. The only thing the kings had wrong was the direction. Eternity is not piled upward. It is laid down one afternoon at a time.
H3	Closing the twenty-first door
B	Retrace the passage and the evening wind on the hill comes out to meet you. The western sky is red again today, and the shadow of the pyramid runs long down the slope. Twenty-one doors close here. We flew above the clouds and took the night train, we sat on the floor of an empty swimming pool, we walked a rainforest and a white wood, we sailed between stars, we sat in a yellow noon and on a snowfield in midsummer, we borrowed a cell in a beehive, we boarded a ship, we passed through an empty granary, and at last we spent an afternoon in the room of forever. And we found out that the best experience, the one we went looking for everywhere, was never inside eternity at all. It was always waiting for us this afternoon, behind one door, for the price of a cup of coffee. The place where the best experience is. We call it the best café.
